# Shorts script

## Pick a shape
| Shape | Length | Ends on | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Standalone vindication | 55-67s | The payoff, shown on screen | Tyre grooves, 1.5M views in 77 h, 66 s |
| B. Part 1 cut | 30-38s | Mid-sentence, just before the payoff. Caption: "FOLLOW FOR PART 2" | Roller-coaster loop, 2.5M views, 31 s |
| C. Series episode | 50-60s | An impossible image, then a PART card | Bunker series EP01-03; the pilot also used a stickman Part 1 that got 21.5M views |

Default to A or B, built on a true hidden reason: a real invention or design that looks wrong and is secretly right.

## Pace and length
- One male narrator, continuous from frame 1. No intro, outro or "like and subscribe".
- Pace is about 3.1-3.6 words/s, which the user preferred over faster. That's about 105-120 words for 32 s and 170-200 words for 60 s.
- Never pad a script to hit 60 s. Length follows engagement.
- When you cut a line, re-time every later beat at the same pace.

## Beat formula
1. **Hook (sentence 1, 8-18 words).** One hero does one absurd act, ideally with a deadline: "He cut grooves into four brand new racing tires the night before the biggest race of the year."
   - Frame 1 shows that act, never the normal setup.
   - Never open on a group or a routine. "Every day, prisoners vaulted a gym horse" flopped at 2.6K.
2. **"You see," by about 3 s gives STAKES, not science.** "It's the first ride ever to go upside down, and it cost him everything."
3. **Escalation (3-6 beats).** Rivals and bystanders mock in short spoken lines ("You just cut away our grip!"). Add a concrete scoreboard ("By lap 50, it's a whole lap behind"). Keep it to at most 3 mockery gags in a row.
4. **A visible disaster by about 10 s.** It should be funny and on screen: "test dummy comes back crushed like a soda can".
5. **One deflating joke.** "Does he know what he's doing? Not really."
6. **One quiet tell or "Wait...?" line.** "He's not even watching the race. He's watching the clouds."
7. **The mechanism is ONE line and ONE picture, about 2 s.** For shape A, put it inside the payoff as an analogy ("like a wet glass sliding on a table"). For shapes B and C, put it before the climax, never after the disaster.
8. **The ending.**
   - Shape A: show the rival failing.
   - Shape B: cut mid-sentence before anything the viewer hasn't seen yet.
   - Shape C: show something impossible, then a ≤1 s PART card.
   - In every shape, don't narrate what's on screen at the end. Let picture and sound carry it.

Sentences should be about 13 words, plain spoken English with contractions.

## Swipe killers (each one was a real QA rejection, with the second it happened)
| Rule | Cost when broken |
|---|---|
| No stat cards or silent "number" shots. Every beat moves the story or is a joke. | Swiped at 12.5s |
| The threat must be seen, hinted at or mentioned every 5 s. Setup montages over 8 s lose the viewer. | Swiped at 12.5s and 27.8s |
| Nothing calm after the climax: no gauge reading, no relieved smile. | Swiped at 37.5s |
| No explainer longer than one line, and none right after the disaster. | Swiped at 16s, 30.6s and 48.3s |
| No dead-air narration like "he looks at X, then at Y, then at X". | Swiped at 46.2s |
| No narrated choice ("Open it or leave him?") that repeats the previous beat. | Swiped at 37.2s |
| At most 3 tests or gags in a row, and each test must change who we believe. A tie gets swiped. | Swiped at 23.5s and 27.8s |
| A normal or satisfying process shot as the opener reads as a factory clip. | Swiped at 2s |
| On-screen text must never give away the answer ("SMOOTH BALL = SHORT FLIGHT"). | Swiped at 12s |
| Avoid mysteries the viewer has already solved ("every golf ball has dents"). Reframe it as an origin story ("1850s: EVERY GOLF BALL WAS SMOOTH"). | Swiped at 17s |
| Voice and on-screen text must never contradict each other. | Swiped at 8s |
| The cliffhanger must not repeat an outcome we've already seen. Leave open something new. | Watched to the end, still REJECT |
| An off-screen voice that matters needs on-screen text, because many viewers watch muted. | Approval tweak |
| In "which one is real" mysteries, each suspect passes one test, so neither is clearly fake. The tie-breaker is an observation (a clean Gary in an ash world gets zero Geiger clicks). | Approved EP03 |

Things that held viewers:
- A cruel choice that creates moral tension, such as shutting the door on the neighbor.
- Ironic reversals, such as "Turns out dirt does stop a nuke."
- Recurring props across episodes: the mug, the clown horn, the sign.

## Visual writing rules (the renderer builds what you write)
- Every sentence must be literally drawable as one clear picture.
- Props carry the jokes. Give each gag a close-up.
- Use at most 4 locations and 4 characters. Give each character one instant trait (hat, glasses, robe, prop).
- Put text on signs in the world (shopfront, poster, scoreboard, sticky note, crowd banner) rather than overlays.
- Use continuous camera moves (follow an object, whip pan, fly through a window or hatch). Keep it to 1-2 hard cuts.
- Light follows the story: noon for hope, sunset for tension, night-blue for mystery, red or white for disaster.
- Check continuity against earlier episodes: who has which injury, and what each sign said.

## Topic picking
- Pick a real design or invention whose "wrong-looking" choice is secretly right: dimpled golf balls, grooved tyres, teardrop loops, the backwards high jump.
- Prefer topics with a real historical hook for the crude fix. For example, makers of early gutta-percha golf balls really did hammer dents into them.
- Part-N episodes continue the existing cast, props and sets. A standalone short gets a fresh cast of at most 4.
- In a standalone Part 1, keep the rival's copy and the sabotage logically separate from the hero's version. Keep the final outcome open, because the rival's version is flawed too.
- Never copy lines from the reference video word for word. Paraphrase the joke.

## Facts
- Web-search every real-world claim before writing, including physics, survival and history claims. Also check technical props shown on screen; for example, a Geiger counter does click faster near fallout dust.
- The story may be invented around a true mechanism. Every number, date and name must be sourced.
- Use the strongest claim the sources support, and no stronger. Write "less than half as far", not "half as far".
- Where sources disagree, keep the claim out of the narration. The dimple patent is dated 1905 in some sources and 1908 in others, so no year is stated.
- Cite 2-5 sources with URLs. Prefer primary or institutional sources (NASA, Scientific American, Britannica). Wikipedia is fine for history.
- End every script with FACT NOTES, listing:
  - what is sourced
  - what is invented (characters, props)
  - what is only a joke ("wobble like potatoes")
  - which details from earlier episodes you assumed
  The coordinator uses this list to catch continuity errors.

## Timing
- Compute word counts and beat times in code (`words / rate`). Never estimate them by hand.
- Use the rate in the current brief. The default is 3.3-3.6 words/s.
- The length in the current brief always beats any older length rule.
- If a target is missed (for example "You see," lands at 3.4 s instead of by 3 s), flag it in the reply. Don't hide it.

## Caption and title
- The caption is the title only, or the title plus "FOLLOW FOR PART 2". The 1.5M reference used no hashtags.
- Title formulas: "Why [normal thing] [is weird]?", "The [odd thing] [twist]", or "He [odd act] on Purpose".
